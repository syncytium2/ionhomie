Mockup for Tony: the simple start. One carbonic anhydrase as a glob, after Domsic et al. 2008: the whole protein with its cleft (their Fig. 1a), CO₂ held on the oily green surface and HCO₃⁻ on the watery side (Fig. 4a), and a chain of waters on that side (Fig. 1). Molecules are drawn far larger than the protein would make them. The order is simplified: in the real enzyme the water is already on the zinc and gives up its H⁺ in a separate half of the cycle. The detailed version is enzyme.html, kept for later.
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ CA H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
CO₂ water HCO₃⁻ H⁺, a lone proton zinc oily pocket

Carbonic anhydrase holds a CO₂ and a water in its cleft, side by side, beside a zinc. It turns them into one HCO₃⁻ and one H⁺, lets them go, and is ready for the next pair: up to about a million times a second.

Press Backward and the same enzyme does the same steps in reverse: HCO₃⁻ and H⁺ go in, CO₂ and water come out. The enzyme does not choose a direction; which way it runs depends on what reaches it.

Domsic JF et al. (2008) Entrapment of carbon dioxide in the active site of carbonic anhydrase II. J Biol Chem 283:30766–30771. doi:10.1074/jbc.M805353200
