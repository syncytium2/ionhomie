Now put a voltage across the box. Water still jostles every ion at random, but a charged ion also feels a steady push from the field: cations toward the negative side, anions toward the positive. A neutral molecule feels nothing.

Predict first. With the left side positive, where do K⁺ and Cl⁻ pile up? What does the neutral molecule do? And Ca²⁺, with twice the charge: the same pile-up as K⁺, a steeper one, or a shallower one?

The box is 2 µm across, slowed about 800×. The plates hold the voltage you set.

Read the voltage back from the ions

Fit each species' pile-up and ask what voltage would hold exactly that gradient. That is the Nernst equation, run backward.

Two fluxes, for K⁺

Through the middle of the box: drift, pushed by the field, and diffusion, pushed back down the gradient that drift builds. The pile-up stops growing when they cancel. Zero net flux is the whole of the Nernst equation.

Concentration across the box

Lines: the particles, counted in slices and smoothed. Dashed: the Boltzmann prediction, c(x) ∝ exp(−zFφ(x)/RT), each scaled to its own mean.

The math: from drift and diffusion to Nernst

The Nernst–Planck equation adds drift in the field to Fick's diffusion:

J = −D ( dc/dx + (zF/RT) · c · dφ/dx )

Set J = 0 and integrate across the box:

ln(c_right / c_left) = (zF/RT) · (φ_left − φ_right) φ_left − φ_right = (RT/zF) · ln(c_right / c_left) ← the Nernst equation

One simplification: in a real solution, piling up charge like this would build its own field at once, and that field would stop the pile-up almost before it began. These ions are treated as dilute test charges in a field set from outside. The compartment chapters put that self-field back, as the charge on the membrane.
