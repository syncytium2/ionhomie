Wrap a membrane around some of the solution and you have a cell: two compartments, each well mixed, each with its own concentrations. This cell already has the gradients of a healthy neuron, K⁺ high inside, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ high outside. Inside it are also large anions that can never cross (A⁻): proteins, nucleic acids, metabolites.

For now the membrane is closed. Nothing crosses it. But it can still hold charge, like a capacitor: a thin film of excess cations on one face and anions on the other. That film is the membrane potential.

Predict first. To charge this membrane to −74 mV, about where a resting neuron sits, roughly what fraction of the cell's K⁺ has to cross it: a tenth, a hundredth, or much less?

Each compartment is neutral

The bulk of each compartment carries no net charge (electroneutrality). The excess that sets Vm is so small it does not show in these sums at all. Equal osmolarity inside and out means no water moves: remember the trapped A⁻; they will matter.

The math: the membrane as a capacitor
Q = C · Vm C = c_m · area, c_m ≈ 1 µF/cm² ions = Q / e

For a sphere 10 µm in radius the area is 1.26 × 10⁻⁵ cm², so C ≈ 12.6 pF. Charging it by 100 mV takes 1.26 pC: about 7.8 million monovalent ions. The cell holds about 3.5 × 10¹¹ K⁺ at 140 mM. The ratio is about 1 in 45,000.
