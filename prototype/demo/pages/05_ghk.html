A real membrane is never permeable to just one ion. Give it K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ channels at once and each ion pulls Vm toward its own equilibrium potential, harder the more permeable the membrane is to it. Vm settles where the currents cancel: between the ticks, weighted by permeability. That point is given by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation.

Predict first. At rest a neuron is about 25 times more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺. Does Vm sit on E_K? If not, how far off? And once Vm has settled, have the ions stopped moving?

Steady, not at equilibrium

The math

Each ion's flux follows the GHK flux equation. Setting the total current to zero gives the GHK voltage equation (for monovalent ions; Cl⁻ enters with inside and outside swapped because its charge is negative):

RT P_K[K]o + P_Na[Na]o + P_Cl[Cl]i Vm = ── ln ───────────────────────────────── F P_K[K]i + P_Na[Na]i + P_Cl[Cl]o

With one permeability set to zero it collapses to the other ions' terms; with only one ion left, it is that ion's Nernst equation.

The zero-current condition says nothing about each ion's flux. At rest, Na⁺ enters and K⁺ leaves without stopping. This is a steady state, not an equilibrium, and a steady state with continuing fluxes has to be paid for. The next chapter asks what happens if it is not.

The squid concentrations (inside K⁺ 400, Na⁺ 50, Cl⁻ 40 mM; outside K⁺ 20, Na⁺ 440, Cl⁻ 560 mM), the squid temperature of 6.3 °C, and Hodgkin and Katz's (1949) resting permeability ratio of 1 : 0.04 : 0.45 are recalled, not yet checked against the sources. The squid preset uses them.
