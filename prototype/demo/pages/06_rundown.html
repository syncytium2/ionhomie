What you just watched

  • The gradients run down. Na⁺ floods in, K⁺ leaks out, and both equilibrium potentials collapse toward zero, taking Vm with them. In this 10 µm cell the collapse takes tens of minutes; a cell with more membrane per volume, or leakier membrane, goes faster.
  • Cl⁻ follows. As Vm depolarizes, Cl⁻ is no longer held out, and it flows in with the Na⁺.
  • The cell swells, and does not stop. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in exceed K⁺ out, and water follows the solute. With impermeant A⁻ trapped inside, no balance is possible: any distribution of the permeable ions that is electrochemically at rest (the Donnan equilibrium) leaves more particles inside than out, so water keeps coming. A real cell would burst; this model does not. The cell drawn above is clamped at a size that still fits.
Clinical connection: ischemia

Cut the blood supply and the cell's ATP runs out within minutes, and with it every pump. What follows is this page: gradients collapse, neurons depolarize, and cells take up Na⁺, Cl⁻ and water and swell (cytotoxic edema). This page shows the mechanism, not the time course of any real tissue: the parameters are illustrative.

The model

A single compartment with leaks for K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (GHK flux equation; PNa/PK = 0.04, PCl/PK = 0.1), a fixed bath, and impermeant A⁻ inside. Vm is found at every step as the voltage where the net current is zero; it changes far faster than the concentrations do. Water follows instantly, so the volume always makes the inside isosmotic with the bath. Concentrations change by the leak fluxes, integrated over seconds.
